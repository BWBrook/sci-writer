# Writing the prose

For the lead author at stage 5, and for anyone editing a draft.

## One worked example

This shows the mechanics. Barry has not yet read it, and its numbers come from an unchecked draft.

The working agents' paragraph:

> Vascular plants can be older if early fossils were scarcer, or recognisable later. At a steady
> recording rate, the 95% older limit on their body record is about 429 Ma, or 432–434 Ma if the
> finds are counted as fewer independent places. A retrospective scenario that halves the earlier
> rate reaches only 431.0 Ma. Dates of 444–452 Ma fit if such fossils were found 8 to 12 times less
> often before the oldest find than after it (2.4 to 4.5 times if counted cautiously), or at a
> steady rate if recognisable bodies appeared at least 15–23 million years after the ancestor.

The outline line:

> [110 words] Reader's question: how much older than its first fossil could the group be? Point: not
> much, unless its early members were rarely found. | numbers allowed: two million years; 20 million
> years; ten times | left in Table 2: 429, 432–434, 431.0, 2.4–4.5, 15–23

The paragraph written from that line:

> Vascular plants could have lived long before their oldest fossil only if their early members were
> rarely found. After that first appearance, their fossils turn up at a steady pace. Had they been
> found at the same pace earlier, any history longer than about two million years would very
> probably have left a find, and none is known. An origin 20 million years earlier, which some
> estimates from DNA propose, therefore requires the earliest vascular plants to have been found
> about ten times less often than their descendants. Table 2 gives the limit under each way of
> counting the finds.

Six of the nine figures stayed in the table. The paragraph makes one inference, and each sentence
gives the reader what the next one needs.

## The whole text

- One question and one main message. A reader could repeat the argument to a colleague.
- Give the answer early and return to it at the close. The message appears in the title, the
  abstract, the opening of the discussion and the close, in different words, and the text never
  remarks on its own repetition.
- An article is an argument and the supplement holds the inventory. What you leave out remains on
  record in the ledger and the guide for agents.
- State a view: what was expected, what was surprising, what the authors think it means. Take these
  from the working agents' account in the pack. Enthusiasm shows in what you choose to tell the
  reader.

## Paragraphs

- One job each. Open with the point and stop when the job is done.
- Begin from something the reader has just read.
- Point first, then its condition, then the number.
- Few numbers: usually one or two that the reader should remember, rounded unless the precision
  matters. An estimate and its interval count as one. Exact values belong in tables. A paragraph
  that needs no number should have none.
- The concrete thing before the abstract one. Say what the object is and what was counted before
  interpreting it. One running example, returned to when the argument turns technical, serves
  better than several.

## By section

- **Introduction.** What is unknown, why it matters, what was done and found. Only the literature
  needed to show the gap.
- **Methods.** Complete and in the order of the procedure. The limits on numbers do not apply here.
  Name each treatment by what it does.
- **Results.** State the pattern in words, then point to the figure or table for values. Most
  important result first, unless the reader needs a short prerequisite to interpret it. Do not
  describe every panel.
- **Discussion.** Open with what the findings mean. One short passage on limitations.

## Sentences

- Keep the subject close to its verb, and let the verb name the action.
- Begin with what the reader already has. Put the new and important thing where the sentence
  closes.
- One main claim to a sentence.
- Say who did what: "we counted" in place of "counts were obtained". A passive is right when it
  keeps the topic first.
- Complete every comparison: higher than what, less often than when.
- Follow "this" with its noun.
- State what was found. Deny something only when the reader is likely to believe it. Never turn a
  failure to detect a difference into a statement that two things are equal.

## Words

- One name for each thing, held from the abstract to the last table.
- Ordinary words. A term the readers already own needs no definition; a term they lack is taught
  once, in a plain sentence, where it first appears; a project's private label stays out.
- The literal phrase where one exists. An analogy earns its place only by explaining what the
  literal phrase cannot.
- Introduce every quantity before using it: what is counted, and against what.
- Replace a hedge with the condition or the size of the uncertainty, where known. Keep a hedge that
  carries the scope of a claim.

## Devices that AI writers overuse

Each is a legitimate device made tiresome by overuse. Use one where it does something for the
reader that the plain sentence would not; a second use on a page needs a better reason. Do not
strip them all out: a real contrast still needs a contrast.

Denial before the claim; announcing importance ("what matters is"); an abstraction as the actor
("the record holds"); a pointer before the thing it points to; a heading that teases; a maxim at the
end of a paragraph; a wrong reading raised to be dismissed; a run of fragments; a metaphor where a
literal phrase exists; a bold label before a sentence. Words to watch: "quiet", "silence" for an
absence, the suffix "-shaped".

## A test for any sentence

Could you say it aloud to a colleague without it sounding like a performance?

## The brief

Two A4 pages for anyone who finds the repository: a journalist, a student, a scientist from another
field. The main finding comes first. Assume no technical term; teach at most a handful, and prefer
dropping an idea to defining another term. Few findings, each made concrete. No claim IDs in the
text the reader sees.
