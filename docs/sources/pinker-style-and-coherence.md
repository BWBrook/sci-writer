## A Window onto the World

**What it says**

- Treat the reader as a capable equal. Start with the phenomenon or question. Describe what someone could observe, then explain the inference. A familiar example can explain an abstract idea if it preserves the relationship that matters. Pinker illustrates this with Brian Greene’s explanations of cosmology. [Chapter 2, lines 443–461 and 465–507](library/guides/pinker-2014-sense-of-style.md:443)
- Keep previews, summaries and accounts of researchers’ activity where they help the reader understand the subject. Order the material so the next step makes sense. When a summary is needed, write it as a self-contained account using enough of the original terminology for readers to recognise the connection. (Lines 551–600.)
- Replace vague hedges with the condition, effect size or degree of certainty the reader needs. State a scientific hypothesis precisely at least once. Generic declarations that a question is difficult or controversial do little to explain what is uncertain. (Lines 602–610, 628–636.)
- Use verbs to say who did what when the action first matters. A passive can keep attention on the entity under discussion or omit an immaterial actor. A noun that names an action can refer back to an event already described, as Chapter 5 explains. (Lines 661–681, 711–721, 768–774 and 1959.)

**Quotations**

- “convey the magnitude of the effect and the degree of certainty explicitly” (Line 634.)

**Bearing on our problem**

This addresses the defensive sentences, private vocabulary, lack of observable detail and openings organised around the research process. For the fossil brief, describe what a sample contains and what researchers counted before interpreting an absence. Preserve any condition that changes the inference.

**Conflicts**

Pinker recommends fresh figurative language more freely than Barry’s comments on the fossil brief warrant. The project’s moderation decision allows an analogy with a specific explanatory purpose. Classic style also assumes a cooperative reader and gives the writer freedom over length and form. The article still has to make its evidence and reasoning assessable, and the brief has two pages. Pinker treats the appearance of fully formed thought as a feature of the finished prose, which is compatible with Barry’s staged drafting method. (Lines 447–461, 543–547, 640–657. [Founding note, lines 97–122](docs/founding-note.md:97), [decisions, lines 35–47](docs/decisions.md:35).)

**For an agent**

1. Mark the first sentence that names the phenomenon and the first that gives an observation or concrete example. Flag any opening paragraph spent solely on the literature, workflow or document structure.
2. For each headline claim, identify the wording that carries its scope and uncertainty. For every hedge removed or replaced, check the revised claim against the ledger.
3. For each retained analogy, state the scientific relationship it explains and where the correspondence ends. Check that the literal claim is recoverable from the prose.

## The Web, the Tree, and the String

**What it says**

- Readers infer sentence structure as they read. Interruptions between a subject and its verb, modifiers placed before a noun in several layers, and clauses embedded inside other unfinished clauses all increase what readers must remember. Put the core clause early, shift complex material towards the end, or split the sentence. Sentence length alone is a poor test. [Chapter 4, lines 1257–1327 and 1333–1409](library/guides/pinker-2014-sense-of-style.md:1257)
- Readers can initially attach a modifier to the wrong phrase or assign a word its more familiar meaning. Reposition the modifier or restore a cue such as *that*, *which*, an article, a preposition or punctuation. Give coordinated items the same grammatical role and use parallel syntax for parallel ideas. (Lines 1153–1183, 1413–1488, 1492–1528, 1544–1578.)
- Put familiar information before new information where that helps the reader follow the passage. A passive or a different verb can keep the familiar subject first and put a longer phrase later. (Lines 1596–1652, 1702–1717.)

**Quotations**

- “Topic, then comment. Given, then new.” (Line 1602.)

**Bearing on our problem**

A writer who knows what a qualifier modifies may overlook a plausible first reading. Review patches can also insert conditions between the subject and verb. Both problems are worth checking in sentences that carry the main findings or specify how something was counted.

**Conflicts**

Barry’s point-first rule works at the scale of a claim or paragraph. Within a sentence, Pinker’s ordering can justify starting with a short, familiar condition. His defence of emphasis constructions also fits the moderation decision when the construction makes clear what the reader should take as given. (Lines 1327, 1600–1608, 1702–1704. [Founding note, lines 186–198](docs/founding-note.md:186), [overused devices, lines 39–46](docs/overused-devices.md:39).)

**For an agent**

1. In each flagged sentence, mark the main subject, verb and intervening clauses. Test a rewrite with fewer interruptions, then verify that every condition still applies to the intended claim.
2. Ask a second reader to identify what each potentially ambiguous modifier qualifies. Where readings differ, reposition it or restore a grammatical cue. In a comparison, check that each item fits the same grammatical construction.

## Arcs of Coherence

**What it says**

- Give the reader the topic and the point early. Group material by purpose and choose an order the reader can follow, such as broad to specific or observation to explanation. Paragraph breaks usually mark a coherent unit or a shift, and their placement can also help readers manage a long passage. (Lines 1777–1795, 1803–1849.)
- Keep recurring entities and themes identifiable. Use stable terms, use pronouns when the referent is clear, and name the entity again when competing referents intervene. Introduce an action before using a noun to refer back to it. (Lines 1865–1917, 1923–1959, 2182–2214.)
- Make the relationship between statements recoverable. Readers need to distinguish explanation from sequence, evidence from example, and the author’s view from someone else’s. Parallel wording helps readers compare like with like. Use a connective where the intended audience needs one and signal the relationship once. (Lines 1963–2056.)
- Use negation when it answers a plausible expectation, and make clear exactly what is denied. Give claims space in proportion to their importance. Substantial counterevidence can have its own section so it can be examined together. (Lines 2066–2168.)

Bransford and Johnson gave groups the same deliberately ambiguous passage about washing clothes, with the topic supplied before, after, or omitted. Recall doubled with the advance cue. The later cue did not improve recall. Pinker gives neither sample size nor participant characteristics. A second passage about flying a kite illustrates ambiguity even when individual sentences are concrete, but Pinker reports no quantitative result for it. The first experiment measures recall of a short passage, with uncertain transfer to scientific articles or reading enjoyment. (Lines 1805–1841.)

Pinker also reports that a school programme teaching students to connect successive ideas in arguments accompanied gains in test scores, graduation and college applications among struggling students. He gives no sample size or comparison design. The account cannot isolate the effect of teaching connectives from the rest of the programme. (Lines 2054–2056.)

**Quotations**

- “When in doubt, connect.” (Line 2042.)

**Bearing on our problem**

This chapter most directly addresses accretion. A paragraph added in revision can be accurate and still obscure which result matters or why the next paragraph follows. Stable names also help readers distinguish a recurring quantity from a new quantity or a subset.

**Conflicts**

Pinker supports Barry’s main-message outline and gives the writer more latitude over paragraph boundaries than exact correspondence to the outline would allow. He accepts an example before or after the generalisation, and advises giving away the point early. The founding note’s question-first, answer-last pattern works if the close returns to an answer the reader has already been given. (Lines 1777–1795, 1843–1849, 2001–2009. [Barry’s methods summary, lines 8–13](docs/methods/README.md:8), [founding note, lines 66–77](docs/founding-note.md:66).)

**For an agent**

1. Ask a reader unfamiliar with the project to state the question and main answer from the opening. Compare both with the intended message before showing them the rest.
2. After substantive revision, record each paragraph’s job and how it follows from the preceding one. Flag duplicated jobs, missing premises and paragraphs whose length gives a secondary result disproportionate attention.
3. For each headline comparison, record both populations, metrics, denominators and units. Check that the wording varies only where the evidence varies.

## Across these sources

The chapters agree that readers need relevant context before they can interpret a sentence, a quantity or a result. They also explain why rules based on counts will miss important cases. Chapter 2 discourages previews that repeat the contents list. Chapter 5 calls for an explicit link when readers could reasonably infer the wrong relation. Chapter 4 shows that restoring a word can make a sentence easier to read. Each decision depends on what the reader needs at that point.

I would drop the founding note’s inference that a failed cold model read establishes failure for a human reader. A cold read can identify the particular term, inference or transition to inspect. Barry’s reading remains the project’s evidence for whether the revision works. Likewise, Barry’s methods summary identifies the final expansion as the point where numbers and caveats return. Checking the expanded draft against the outline should include what each addition does to the surrounding argument, alongside whether it is individually true. ([Founding note, lines 213–229](docs/founding-note.md:213), [methods summary, lines 22–27](docs/methods/README.md:22).)

Two of Pinker’s own edits show why that truth check matters. In Chapter 5, the news-site example treats a share of visits as comparable to a share of the 50 most visited sites. His infant example turns a result described as statistically non-significant into equality of looking times. Both revisions read more easily and change the scientific claim. (Lines 1992–1999 and 2078–2084.)
