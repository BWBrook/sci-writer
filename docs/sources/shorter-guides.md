## Zhang, *Ten Simple Rules*

**What it says**

- Outline the paper while planning the research. Revisit the outline when writing exposes a weak inference or a missing analysis. Choose the material that makes one coherent, adequately supported argument. Order figures and tables by what the reader needs to establish first. [Rules 1–4](library/guides/zhang-2014-ten-simple-rules-writing-research-papers.md:5)
- Choose the audience and journal before deciding how much background to give. Define uncommon terms, state important comparisons explicitly and make captions intelligible on their own. Use the prose to explain what displays establish. Retain the details needed for reproducibility and put secondary material in the supplement. (Rules 3, 5–6)
- Set the draft aside, then read it literally and scrutinise the reasoning. Seek criticism from colleagues before submission. When reviewers identify a problem, determine whether it requires clearer writing, a new analysis or more evidence. (Rules 8–10)

**Quotations**

“extract the meaning literally from the language as written” (Rule 8)

**Bearing on our problem**

The display sequence gives the writer a way to order results by the argument and identify which ones can move out of the main text. Explicit comparisons and definitions address private terminology and unexplained quantities. Rule 10 allows a substantive repair or a fresh rewrite when a local qualification would leave the problem in place.

**Conflicts**

Zhang treats the researcher as the best referee of their own work and encourages anticipating reviewers’ objections. The project assigns reader editing to a second model and scientific checking to the analysts. Zhang’s advice fits the scientific check. Pinker’s representative reader is better placed to detect missing explanations. Zhang’s encouragement to vary declarative sentences also needs Barry’s restraint.

**For an agent**

1. List the main-text displays in order and record the claim each establishes and any earlier result it depends on.
2. Flag each comparison whose magnitude or direction the reader has to calculate. Check each caption for the groups, units and quantities needed to read the display.
3. For each review comment, record whether it changes the evidence, the claim or its explanation. Check the revised argument against that decision.

## Bates College, *Journal-style paper guide*

**What it says**

- Draft the introduction backwards from the specific purpose to identify which background the reader needs. Explain why the study design or model system answers the question. (pp. 5–7)
- Present each experiment or procedure as a unit even if its steps occurred at different times. Label treatments by what they received. Cite standard methods where appropriate and describe what changed. [Methods, pp. 7–10](library/guides/bates-college-2008-how-to-write-journal-style-paper.txt:314)
- Identify one or two findings per display before drafting Results. State the substantive difference or trend, with the statistical test as support. In Discussion, recall a result in the sentence that interprets it. (pp. 12–15)

**Quotations**

None.

**Bearing on our problem**

Descriptive treatment names remove shorthand that otherwise forces readers to look back. Writing the substantive comparison first gives statistical detail a clear purpose. Planning the introduction from the question makes it easier to remove background added simply because the team read it.

**Conflicts**

Bates says to write the abstract last. Barry’s methods and Brown start with a working abstract. Bates’s final agreement check is compatible with that sequence. Bates also puts the purpose near the end of the introduction and keeps interpretation out of Results. That differs from the founding note’s first-paragraph question, Zhang’s advice to interpret displays in Results and Brown’s flexibility about section boundaries.

**For an agent**

1. Check treatment labels in the Methods, Results and displays. Flag opaque labels wherever a reader needs to know the treatment to interpret a comparison.
2. For each main display, record the one or two findings conveyed in prose and flag repeated values that serve no additional purpose.
3. Match each finding in the final abstract to a passage in the body. Check that the population, contrast and conclusion agree.

## Nature, *Summary paragraph*

**What it says**

Give scientists from any discipline enough context to recognise the subject, then give readers in related fields enough background to understand the problem. State the problem and the main result in one sentence each. Explain what the result changes or adds to prior knowledge, then give its wider significance. An optional broader perspective can take this first paragraph up to 300 words if the editor judges that it substantially improves accessibility. The guide illustrates the format with a published example. [Annotated example, p. 1](library/guides/nature-summary-paragraph.pdf)

**Quotations**

None.

**Bearing on our problem**

The result sentence forces a choice about what matters most. The comparison with previous knowledge gives the authors a specific way to explain why it matters. The example itself starts with specialist terms, so following the prescribed sequence still leaves the writer with work to do on accessibility.

**Conflicts**

Nature prescribes a sequence and gives sentence counts. Brown allows the answers in a working abstract to come in different orders, and Barry uses the abstract to develop the argument through successive drafts. Nature’s intended reader is a scientist. The brief needs to work for readers with less background, and its two-page limit governs the whole document.

**For an agent**

1. Mark the sentences that state the problem, main result, advance over previous knowledge and wider significance. Flag a missing function or competing sentences that state different main results.
2. Give the opening to a reader from the intended audience and ask them to identify the subject and what the study found. Record the terms that prevent either answer.

## Pierce, *The Curse of Knowledge*

**What it says**

- Choose a real person with the minimum expertise you expect. Explain the main problem and answer early. State specific contributions that someone could challenge, including why each matters. Introduce a simple example and return to it when the argument becomes technical. [Slides 9–20](library/guides/pierce-2017-curse-of-knowledge-plmw-slides.pdf)
- Explain how previous studies relate to one another and to yours. Use brief references to the question or example where a reader needs to know why a section is there. Read aloud and get actual readers. (Slides 21–25)
- Cut prose that does no work. Link neighbouring sentences by starting from information the reader already has and placing the new information where it can be understood. Passive voice can preserve that order. The slides demonstrate these techniques and offer practical advice. (Slides 27–34, 47)

**Quotations**

“Topic, then comment. Given, then new.” (Slide 30, quoting Pinker)

**Bearing on our problem**

A recurring concrete example addresses the founding note’s complaint that there is nothing to picture. A short list of contributions gives the writer a basis for selecting which results to include as new analyses arrive. Sentence order can also make references clear without adding definitions or signposting everywhere.

**Conflicts**

Pierce’s preference for given information before new information can pull against Barry’s point-first rule if applied sentence by sentence. A paragraph can open with its point in terms the reader already knows. Pierce quotes a suggestion to try cutting 40%. The slides give no grounds for making that a target for every draft.

**For an agent**

1. Record the intended reader’s assumed knowledge. Flag any contribution that depends on knowledge outside that list without explaining it.
2. Match each stated contribution to the evidence that establishes it. Flag descriptions of work done that leave the advance unspecified.
3. Identify the opening example and the later passage that uses it. In hard-to-follow paragraphs, mark where a sentence changes subject without establishing the connection.

## Pinker, APS lecture report

**What it says**

- Experts omit definitions, intermediate inferences and physical details because those are already familiar to them. In the tap-perception example, specifying where and when the taps were delivered lets the reader understand why later taps could alter the perceived locations of earlier ones. The report describes the procedure and outcome qualitatively and gives no sample size. [Lines 18–34](library/guides/pinker-curse-of-knowledge-aps-observer-report.txt:18)
- The report describes a false-belief task in which young children discover pencils in a sweets box and are asked what another child would expect and what they themselves expected before opening it. It reports that children attribute their new knowledge to both the other child and their earlier selves. It gives no sample size, age breakdown or response rates. The task measures attribution of knowledge in children. The application to expert writing is Pinker’s interpretation. (Lines 10–20)
- Show an early draft to a representative reader. Revise for intelligibility with the substantive content fixed. Prefer concrete description and explicit reasoning, and give grammar conventions less attention than coherence and factual accuracy. (Lines 36–44)

**Quotations**

“You’d often be surprised to find that what’s obvious to you is not obvious to anyone else” (line 38)

**Bearing on our problem**

This is the closest diagnosis of Barry’s reaction to the Silurian paragraph. Familiar words such as *rate* can leave the object, denominator or comparison unspecified. A reader who can reconstruct what was measured and how it supports the conclusion can also judge whether another explanation fits.

**Conflicts**

Pinker questions the value of imagining a reader’s knowledge without checking it. Pierce uses an imagined explanation as a drafting aid and also asks for actual readers. Pinker’s advice to remove habitual hedges needs a scientific check where uncertainty changes the scope or strength of a claim.

**For an agent**

1. Give a reader without project context the draft alone. Ask what was done, what was found and how the finding supports the conclusion. Record the first passage where an answer requires project knowledge.
2. Revise the flagged passages for intelligibility. Compare the claims before and after, including their scope and uncertainty.

## Brown, Pressland and Rogers, *Righting Scientific Writing*

**Conflicts**

Brown and Barry both start with a working abstract, in tension with Bates’s instruction to write it last. Brown allows material to appear where the reader needs it and recommends drafting Results and Discussion separately before combining them. Bates is stricter about what belongs in each section. Brown also asks for the main answer in the introduction and for the message to recur at strategic points. That puts pressure on any reading of the founding note that delays the answer until the end. [pp. 186–188](library/guides/brown-pressland-rogers-1993-righting-scientific-writing.md:177)

**For an agent**

1. Write the main message in at most 25 words. Compare the title, abstract, introduction and opening of the discussion against it. Flag changes in scope, certainty or emphasis.
2. Record which material the outline leaves out and why. If a review changes the main message, update the working abstract and recheck the outline before editing individual paragraphs.
3. If combining Results and Discussion, draft the interpretations separately and verify that each survives beside the result it explains. (pp. 184, 186–188)

## Across these sources

- **Agreement.** Choose the intended reader and the main message early. Select evidence and background according to what that reader needs to understand and judge the claim. Give a representative reader a chance to show where the explanation fails.
- **Differences.** Bates is useful for checks within conventional journal sections. Brown is more willing to place information where the argument needs it, and Pierce is more explicit about giving readers the contributions early. Nature supplies a sequence for one journal’s summary paragraph. Barry’s expanding abstract supplies a way to develop the whole argument. An agent can use both, with the final format determined by the venue and audience.
- **The project’s cold-read proposal overstates what a model’s failure establishes.** Use a project-naive model to locate gaps, and check comprehension and enjoyment separately with readers from the intended audiences. A reader can accurately repeat a claim and still find the draft tedious. These sources offer editorial experience and illustrations. The proposed workflow still needs testing on agent-written drafts.
- **The last expansion needs a content check.** The methods README records that numbers and caveats returned when the compressed draft became full prose. Give each newly added result, quantity or qualification a reason to appear there. Keep qualifications that change how the reader should take a headline finding close enough to govern its interpretation.
