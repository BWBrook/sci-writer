## Gopen and Swan (1990)

**What it says**

- Readers use position to infer what a passage is about and which information matters. A table ordered by time, with time at the left and temperature at the right, gives the reader the context before the result. Recognisable article sections likewise tell readers what to expect. (PDF pp. 1–2, “Writing with the Reader in Mind”)
- Put the main verb close to its subject and use that verb to name the action. Move important material that interrupts the subject and verb into a clause of its own. Omit the interruption if it serves no purpose. Give each unit one main job. (pp. 2–3, “Subject-Verb Separation” and “The Stress Position”, p. 6, “Locating the Action”)
- Open a sentence with the entity it concerns and, where possible, information that connects it to the preceding text. Put the consequential new information where the syntax comes to a close, often at the end of a clause or sentence. A passive construction can help when it keeps the established subject of discussion first. Sentence length alone is a poor guide. (pp. 2–5, “The Stress Position” and “The Topic Position”)
- Reordering sentences can expose reasoning the writer assumed the reader would supply. In three of four worked passages, rearrangement exposed a gap that required adding an explanation or removing material. The authors interpret and revise selected published or accepted passages. They report no independent comparison of reader comprehension or check that their inferred meaning matches what the original authors intended. The principles describe expectations that writers can depart from deliberately. (pp. 5–7, “Perceiving Logical Gaps”, “Locating the Action” and “Writing and the Scientific Process”)

**Quotations**

> “Readers expect the action of a sentence to be articulated by the verb.” (PDF p. 6, “Locating the Action”) [Source](library/guides/gopen-swan-1990-science-of-scientific-writing.pdf)

**Bearing on our problem.** Successive qualifications can separate the subject from its verb or put a secondary detail where readers expect the main result. Opening with the same entity across related sentences helps readers follow a finding through its evidence. The earthquake example also shows why replacing project jargon alone can leave a passage hard to follow. Readers still need to know how one statement supports the next. Naming what the authors measured or inferred can restore some of the authorial presence missing in the founding note.

**Conflicts.** Barry’s point-first preference in the founding note concerns the order of the finding, condition and number. Gopen and Swan sometimes put the consequential information last. A paragraph can state its point early while individual sentences begin with a familiar referent. Their warning about mixing Results and Discussion also differs from Whitesides’ combined format. They deliberately retain specialist vocabulary, so their method addresses only part of the brief’s problem.

**For an agent**

1. For a difficult paragraph, mark the opening referent, main verb and intended emphasis of each sentence. Flag long interruptions between subject and verb and sentence endings that give incidental details the most prominence.
2. Ask a second reader to state how each sentence follows from the previous one. Where the reader has to supply a premise, identify the missing premise and ask a science checker to verify it before the writer adds it.
3. Read the first column and headings of each table on their own. Check whether they give the comparison or ordering needed to interpret the values.

## Whitesides (2004)

**What it says**

- Start the outline during the research and revise it as the evidence changes. List why the work began, what hypotheses were intended, what the data actually test, what was measured and what was learned. Record both the original and later hypotheses. Show where missing evidence would go and what result would support the proposed interpretation. Whitesides describes cycling through outlines with his group, often alongside further experiments. This is laboratory practice reported in an essay. (§§1, 2.1–2.2, pp. 1375–1376)
- Build the outline around the data. Put captioned figures, tables, equations and schemes in order. Sketch alternatives when a display obscures the result. Lead with the most important findings, give secondary results less space and omit them if they contribute little. Use specific topic or finding headings and put experimental subsections in the order in which readers encounter the corresponding results. (§§2.2–2.4, pp. 1376–1377)
- Write out the first paragraph or two of the Introduction early. State the objective and why it matters, give relevant background and tell the reader what to look for. Leave the abstract until the paper is complete. Use the conclusion to interpret the findings and explain their significance. (§2.3, pp. 1376–1377)
- Make comparisons explicit, name the referent of a demonstrative, unpack noun strings, describe experimental results in the past tense and favour active voice. His spacing and formatting instructions belong to his group’s chemistry workflow. (§3, p. 1377)

**Quotations**

> “An outline is a written plan of the organization of a paper, including the data on which it rests.” (§2.1, p. 1375) [Source](library/guides/whitesides-2004-writing-a-paper.pdf)

**Bearing on our problem.** A display-based outline gives the writer a way to rank results and keep exact values available without transcribing them into prose. Starting it early also gives new analyses and reviewer requests a place to be assessed against the main question. His advice on comparisons addresses the founding note’s undefined rates and quantities. A reader needs to know what was measured and what serves as the reference.

**Conflicts.** Barry’s methods start with a working abstract. Whitesides writes the abstract last. An early abstract can serve as a planning document, with the publication abstract written once the evidence and argument are stable. Whitesides leads with the most important results, while Mensh and Kording order them by what the reader must establish first. Those orders differ when a control or validation is necessary to interpret the headline. His preference for active voice also differs from Gopen and Swan’s emphasis on keeping the topic in the subject position.

**For an agent**

1. Make an outline that lists each main finding, its supporting display and the inference that display permits. Mark any missing evidence and distinguish hypotheses formed before and after examining the data.
2. For each secondary result, record what part of the argument would change if it left the main text. Use that answer to decide where to report it.
3. Flag comparative claims and rates whose reference is missing. Require the comparator, or the counted event and denominator, to be recoverable from the adjacent text or display.

## Mensh and Kording (2017)

**What it says**

- Give the paper one defensible contribution and communicate it in the title. Several results or innovations can serve that contribution if their relationship is clear. Write for readers unfamiliar with the project, define necessary terms and limit abbreviations and unresolved references. (Rules 1–2)
- Use context, content and conclusion as the default sequence. The title and abstract already give article readers early access to the result. A news-style opening can suit readers who want the result quickly, though a claim may be hard to assess before the relevant context is given. Keep related material together, use parallel form for comparable points and use one name for each concept. (Rules 3–4)
- Make the abstract self-contained. Establish why the specific question matters, state the approach and main findings, then answer the question. Narrow the Introduction to the gap the paper addresses, using only the literature needed to motivate it, and end with what the study did and found. (Rules 5–6)
- Order Results by the logic of the evidence, including controls and method details where readers need them. Give readers the gist of the approach early. A Results paragraph can pose a question, give the evidence and end with the answer. Use declarative statements for the Results outline, with finding headings or figure titles where appropriate. Figure titles convey the result and legends explain how it was obtained. Open the Discussion with what the findings mean, then assess limitations and strengths in light of the literature and explain what the result makes possible. (Rules 7–8)
- Develop the argument during the research. Draft one informal sentence per planned paragraph and concentrate revision on the title, abstract, figures and outline. Ask readers to explain what they understood and where they lost the argument. Rewrite whole paragraphs when their function changes. The article offers heuristics and diagnostic signs, without a controlled test of the ten rules. (Rules 9–10, Table 1 and “Discussion”)

**Quotations**

> “each paragraph should have a defined role in advancing this story” (Rule 9) [Source](library/guides/mensh-kording-2017-ten-simple-rules-structuring-papers.md:116)

**Bearing on our problem.** The one-sentence outline is close to Barry’s paragraph skeleton. It gives each result a reason to appear and lets the writer check whether the order reflects the argument or the order of analysis. The Introduction’s task is to explain what is unknown and why resolving it matters. That gives the writer a way to express interest in the science through the question itself. The advice to replace whole paragraphs also addresses the return of numbers and caveats during Barry’s final expansion.

**Conflicts.** Rule 7 often puts the answer at the end of a paragraph. The founding note asks each paragraph to open with its point and asks the first paragraph to raise the paper’s question. Rule 6 allows several paragraphs before the precise gap is stated. The order matters most in a short brief. Rule 7 favours declarative Results headings or figure titles. Barry’s decision permits finding headings selectively, and Rule 7 itself allows them to remain in the outline. The worked Discussion in Rule 8 gives several paragraphs to limitations. Barry’s short caveats section asks for more selection.

**For an agent**

1. Write the contribution in one sentence. Ask a reader unfamiliar with the work to state it after reading the title and abstract, then compare their answer with the intended claim.
2. List each Results claim, the figure or table that supports it and any prior result required to interpret it. Check that prerequisites appear before dependent claims.
3. Compare the full draft with the one-sentence-per-paragraph outline. For each added paragraph or substantial qualification, identify the new work it does for the argument. Rewrite a paragraph if its purpose has changed.

## Pinker (2014), chapter 3

**What it says**

- Writers systematically overestimate what other people know. Pinker’s explanation includes two mechanisms. With *chunking*, a familiar term lets an expert recall several ideas at once, while the reader may have to learn them separately. With *functional fixedness*, a familiar object or event is described by its role in the author’s work, leaving the reader unable to tell what physically happened. Even readers in the same discipline can lack the particular knowledge a passage assumes. (Lines 818–830, 848–894)
- Replace private shorthand with ordinary words where the meaning is equivalent. Retain terminology established among the intended readers, expand unfamiliar abbreviations at first use, and prefer short explanations and examples to bare labels. A second example can show which property the first example was meant to illustrate. Describe the observable action or object when it helps the reader assess the inference. (Lines 848–866, 878–882, 888–928)
- Ask people similar to the intended readers to try the draft. Take repeated problems seriously and use judgement on individual comments. Revisiting unfamiliar prose after a delay can expose missing steps. Pinker describes repeated revision before and after feedback. (Lines 934–946)

**Evidence reported in the chapter**

- In a style analysis of 500 journal articles, Helen Sword found lively prose in a minority of articles in each field examined. The chapter gives neither the selection procedure nor the scoring criteria. The result shows that journals publish such prose. It cannot establish how editorial preferences affect its prevalence. (Line 820)
- Experimental volunteers rated how difficult anagrams would be for other people after being shown some answers. They judged anagrams whose answers they had seen to be easier for others. In a separate phone-learning task, experienced users predicted novices would take 13 minutes, less experienced users predicted 20, and novices took 32. The chapter gives no group sizes or uncertainty. Both studies measure judgement of others’ difficulty on specific tasks. (Line 828)
- In the candle task, participants had to mount a candle using matches and a box of tacks. Most missed that the box could serve as a support. The chapter supplies no sample size or comparison-group result, and applying the mechanism to scientific wording is Pinker’s interpretation. He also reports that concrete wording helps comprehension and recall, and that prompts to imagine the reader often fail to correct biased judgements. The chapter summarises these findings without enough design or sample detail to judge their size or generality. (Lines 844–846, 890–904)

**Quotations**

> “The form in which thoughts occur to a writer is rarely the same as the form in which they can be absorbed by a reader.” (Line 946) [Source](library/guides/pinker-2014-sense-of-style.md:946)

**Bearing on our problem.** Pinker explains why a phrase can sound ordinary to the people who made the analysis and still leave outsiders unable to identify what was counted. His account also explains why a literal description of a borehole sample could do more than a definition of a project category. His advice on handling comments supports the agreed process in which reviewers flag problems and one writer decides how to resolve them.

**Conflicts.** Pinker allows terms already familiar to the audience. Applied literally, the founding note’s instruction to define every term imposes a heavier burden, especially in the brief. His own plain-language rewrite of the association between food intake and body mass index reads as an individual causal claim. That is a case for the project’s truth check. Pinker also gives actual reader feedback more weight than imagining a reader. Mensh and Kording include both, but their Rule 2 on its own gives the writer little means of discovering what they have assumed.

**For an agent**

1. List the first occurrence of each project-specific term, abbreviation and measured quantity. For the brief and article separately, check for familiar wording, an adequate first-use explanation or a reason the expected reader already knows it.
2. Ask a reader unfamiliar with the project to describe one concrete observation, the conclusion drawn from it and how the observation supports that conclusion. Record where the reader had to guess.
3. Compare each plain-language rewrite with the original claim for changes in causality, population, comparator, uncertainty or scope. Send discrepancies to the science checker.

## Across these sources

- **Agreement.** Order the text by what a reader needs to understand the claim. Give major results more prominence than supporting detail. Whitesides and Mensh and Kording make that hierarchy visible in the outline and figures. Gopen and Swan make it visible within sentences and paragraphs. Pinker shows how a reader can still fail to follow a well-ordered argument if the writer has assumed the wrong background knowledge.
- **Conflicts.** The abstract can be drafted first as a working statement of the argument or last as a report of the finished paper. Results can lead with the strongest finding or with evidence needed to interpret it. The brief can give the answer early if it supplies enough context to make the answer intelligible. The article has room to establish more of the evidence in order. Headings, paragraph openings and sentence endings offer different places to emphasise a finding. Using all three to repeat the same claim can produce the cadence Barry dislikes.
- **What I would change.** The methods README assigns the failed final expansion to prose. Some of that failure is still structural. Expansion is where the writer decides which evidence, definitions and qualifications the reader needs. Where a qualification changes the scope of the headline finding, it belongs with that finding. Where a premise is essential to understanding an inference, it belongs in the argument. Other detail can stay in a display, supplement or agent guide. The check is whether a reader can recover the intended inference and its limits from the finished draft.
- **What the evidence can establish.** A first reading by a model unfamiliar with the project can reveal a missing definition, a mistaken emphasis or a gap in the reasoning. The founding note goes too far in treating one model’s failure as proof of a human reader’s failure. These sources also do not establish what makes a scientific article or brief enjoyable to its intended readers. Assess comprehension, fidelity to the evidence and willingness to keep reading separately. Barry’s reading can judge all three, and a reader from the brief’s intended audience can test assumptions Barry already shares with the project.
