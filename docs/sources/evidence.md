## Hillier et al. — Narrative style and citations

**What it says**  
An observational study of 732 climate-change abstracts published in 19 journals in 2009–2010. Seven crowd judgements per abstract assessed setting, first-person narration, sensory or emotional language, conjunctions that express logical relationships, links between successive sentences, and appeals to the reader. Sensory language, conjunctions, sentence links and appeals were positively associated with later article citations. Setting and first person showed no clear association. The combined narrative index explained 5% of variation in log citations on its own and was strongly associated with journal impact factor. Journal, topic and author differences could account for some of the associations. The sample excluded uncited papers. The outcome was citation count. (Methods and Results, lines 13–65 and 69–120.) [Source](library/evidence/hillier-2016-narrative-style-citation-frequency.md)

**Quotations**  
“Our study design did not allow us to test the mechanism(s) of association between narrativity and citation frequency.” (Discussion, line 130.)

**Bearing on our problem**  
Sentence links address a specific failure of drafts assembled from separate analyses. The reader needs to know how each result follows from, qualifies or helps explain the preceding one. The sensory-language measure gives a reason to test literal descriptions of objects such as fossils or samples. It provides no test of whether readers understood them better.

**Conflicts**  
Weinberger found that shorter abstracts tended to receive fewer citations. Hillier found no length association after accounting for journal. Hillier’s first-person result gives no citation-based reason to require a visible narrator, one of the concerns in the founding note.

**For an agent**  
For each adjacent pair of sentences in a results paragraph, identify the shared referent or the logical relation. Flag links that a reader must reconstruct from project knowledge. Check that each causal or temporal connective expresses a relation supported by the evidence.

## Weinberger et al. — Ten empirical rules

**What it says**  
An observational analysis of more than one million abstracts across eight disciplines and 17 years. The authors measured 15 features of wording and structure relative to journal norms and modelled citation counts accounting for age, journal, authors and references. Longer abstracts, fewer common or easy words, and more adjectives or adverbs tended to accompany more citations. Short sentences were positively associated with citations only in mathematics and physics. Novelty and importance words, superlatives, and words rated pleasant, active or easy to imagine had positive associations. Keyword overlap had a negative association. Results for tense and hedging varied across fields. These are coarse measures of style. Keyword overlap, for example, does not establish whether an abstract has one main point. The authors proposed discoverability as an explanation and did not measure it. (Rules 1–10, Results and Conclusions, lines 7–85.) [Source](library/evidence/weinberger-2015-ten-simple-empirical-rules-writing-science.md)

**Quotations**  
None selected.

**Bearing on our problem**  
An abstract has to identify the study for people searching for it. Retaining the recognised names of organisms, methods and quantities can serve that purpose. For the brief, an unfamiliar term earns its place when the reader needs the distinction it expresses.

**Conflicts**  
The citation associations for length and easy words run in a different direction from Ryba’s reader outcomes. The positive associations for importance words and superlatives also sit uneasily with Barry’s preference for measured claims. The study supplies no evidence that inserting these words improves communication.

**For an agent**  
List the study entities and methods a searcher would need to identify the article. Check that the abstract names them and that a reader can explain any unfamiliar term that matters to the finding. When an importance word is flagged, identify the specific consequence or comparison that justifies it.

## Ryba et al. — Reader confidence and understanding

**What it says**  
The authors randomly assigned 170 third-year science undergraduates to read variants of abstracts on social anxiety, solar cells and populist politics. Each topic had four versions. Nine features changed together, including length, acronyms, noun clusters, hedges, signposting, punctuation, conjunctions, setting and first person. Readers rated ease and confidence, then answered two multiple-choice questions on the purpose and main finding. From the hardest to the easiest version, rated ease rose from 44% to 66%, confidence from 49% to 62% using Table 2, and correct answers from 48% to 58%. Ease and confidence rose monotonically across the four versions. Correct answers did not, although the fitted trend was positive. The randomisation supports a causal effect of the combined versions for these readers and passages. Three topics and two comprehension questions limit the inference, and the design cannot identify the effect of any individual feature. The Results prose gives different confidence means from Table 2. (Methods, Tables 1–3 and Results, pp. 2–6.) [Source](library/evidence/ryba-2021-better-writing-reader-confidence.pdf)

**Quotations**  
“Why was this research conducted?” and “What is the take-home message of this research?” (Measures, p. 3.)

**Bearing on our problem**  
This directly tests whether readers can identify why a study was done and what it found. It makes acronym density, long noun strings and missing signposts reasonable places to investigate when readers stumble over project shorthand.

**Conflicts**  
The accessible package contained fewer hedges. Centonze shows why removing a hedge can change a claim. The experimental result supports testing the accessible version as a whole while checking which qualifications have to survive.

**For an agent**  
Before showing readers the draft, record acceptable answers for the research question, main finding and its scope. Ask readers for those answers after one reading. Record perceived ease and confidence separately from the proportion of correct answers.

## Feld, Lines and Ross — Writing Matters

**What it says**  
The cover identifies the requested title, authors and year and gives IZA Discussion Paper **15671**. The filename transposes two digits. In a preregistered experiment, professional editors revised 30 economics PhD papers, first addressing structure and then sentences and words, with emphasis on the title, abstract and introduction. The authors checked that the meaning survived. Eighteen writing experts and 30 economists each assessed five original and five edited papers under a randomised block design. Each saw only one version of a given paper. Writing experts found the main message easier to locate by 0.58 SD and the prose easier to read by 0.53 SD. Economists rated overall quality 0.20 SD higher, gave conference acceptance an 8.4 percentage point higher stated probability, and gave publication in a highly ranked journal a 4.1 point higher predicted probability. The design supports a causal effect of the editing package on these ratings. The outcomes were brief assessments of economics papers and hypothetical screening decisions. (Sections 2–3, Tables 2, 4 and 5.) [Source](library/evidence/feld-lines-ross-writing-matters-iza15671.pdf)

**Quotations**  
“Keep a short distance between nouns and their accompanying verb” (Table 2, item 4, p. 9).

**Bearing on our problem**  
The editing guide requires the abstract to give the question, method and main finding, the paragraph to handle one idea, and the text to deliver what it promises. It also calls for familiar words, direct verbs and intelligible display titles. The order of work supports Barry’s diagnosis that repairing the argument and repairing the prose are distinct tasks. The study tested both together.

**Conflicts**  
The authors checked the edited meaning and could propose changes. Barry assigns scientific checkers to report faults and gives the writer control of wording. Feld supports involving a subject expert in checking meaning. It leaves the allocation of final wording untested.

**For an agent**  
Reverse-outline the introduction before polishing sentences. For each paragraph, record its one job and check that it performs it. Mark the question, method and principal finding in the abstract. Send substantive edits to the science checker with the original wording and the relevant evidence.

## APRES — Agentic paper revision

**What it says**  
APRES searches for a model-scored rubric that predicts 12-month Semantic Scholar influential-citation counts, then uses the rubric to guide, score and select iterative revisions. The prediction benchmark used 26,707 papers from four ICLR and NeurIPS meetings, split 80/10/10. The best reported mean absolute prediction error was 1.92. A separate blind comparison used 364 original–revision pairs, with three machine-learning researchers or engineers per pair. A majority preferred the revision in 287 pairs, or 79%. The assessment instructions describe comparisons of excerpts and ask which version is better presented. Actual citations to revisions and preservation of scientific claims were outside that evaluation. The system works on text and omits figures. (Sections 3–5, Table 3 and Appendix G.) [Source](library/evidence/apres-2026-agentic-paper-revision.pdf)

**Quotations**  
None selected.

**Bearing on our problem**  
Search-and-replace edits make changes inspectable and prevent the drastic shortening the authors observed with whole-paper regeneration. That is useful for bounded repairs. An accumulation of locally preferred changes can still leave the argument disordered, and protecting tables leaves the interpretation of their values open to change.

**Conflicts**  
APRES selects revisions by a score derived from the same rubric that guides them. Barry’s process assigns the writer, reader’s editor and scientific checker different responsibilities. Jourdan’s results give a further reason to put little weight on a single model score when choosing between plausible versions.

**For an agent**  
Retain a diff for each proposed repair, with the problem it addresses. Keep scientific tables outside prose edits and check the revised interpretation against them. Compare originals and revisions blind, using an evaluator independent of the score that selected the revision.

## Da Silva et al. — Process-oriented evaluation

**What it says**  
The study reanalysed 869 human editing sessions with 236,033 keystroke-level edits on 45 pairs of computer-science abstracts, one human and one AI version per paper. The original experiment randomised abstract provenance and disclosure. This analysis grouped edits into bursts and measured their effects on 25 computational features covering agency, economy, sentence structure, coherence and framing. AI originals scored higher on agency and structure and lower on coherence. Human revision generally left these differences in place. Substitution and pruning showed the strongest improvements in weak passages on several measures. In a separate comparison on the 90 abstracts, GPT-5.4 and Claude Opus 4.6 with a rubric improved local measures more consistently than discourse measures. Coherence fell among initially high-scoring drafts. In 200 targeted editing tasks, explicit scope instructions reduced spillover, but edits still ran wider than human edits. The outcome was mainly changes in text metrics. Editors selected their own edits, and comparisons by initial score are susceptible to regression to the mean. (Sections 2–4 and 8, Figures 3–4.) [Source](library/evidence/process-oriented-evaluation-2026.pdf)

**Quotations**  
None selected.

**Bearing on our problem**  
The distinction between sentence quality and coherence is central to accretion. A new qualification can improve the sentence that carries it while making the paragraph’s conclusion harder to identify. Counting changes or checking grammatical polish will miss that problem.

**Conflicts**  
Feld found that professional editing improved judges’ assessments. This study found little change after human editing on most of its computational measures. The units, editors, tasks and outcomes differ. It also qualifies APRES’s result. Human preference for revised excerpts can coexist with losses in coherence among already strong abstracts.

**For an agent**  
Record whether each repair adds, removes, replaces or moves material. After several local edits, ask a fresh reader to reconstruct the argument. For an edit intended to touch one passage, use the diff to check whether other passages changed.

## Centonze — Beyond Fluency

**What it says**  
The Claim-Preservation Framework asks whether a rewrite preserves five things: the proposition, degree of certainty, relation to evidence, function in the argument, and qualifications or attribution. A sentence can retain its subject and direction while changing an association into a cause, an observation into an interpretation, or a restricted finding into a general one. The proof of concept used 40 purposively selected SciFact claims, one model and one standard prompt. The author alone annotated the results. Five of the six published examples were preserved. In the sixth, a causal statement became an association despite explicit instructions to preserve its force. The remaining cases were withheld and no aggregate rate was reported. This illustrates a failure mode. The study establishes neither its frequency nor the reliability of the framework across annotators. (Sections 3–5 and 7, Tables 1–3, pp. 135–139.) [Source](library/evidence/beyond-fluency-2026-claim-preservation.pdf)

**Quotations**  
“The criterion is preservation rather than maximization” (Section 4, p. 136).

**Bearing on our problem**  
This makes the truth-checker’s task concrete. Moving a caveat can change the apparent meaning of a headline even if every word of the caveat survives elsewhere. Conversely, adding caution can weaken a conclusion already warranted by the evidence.

**Conflicts**  
The framework limits advice to remove hedges or write more boldly. It also exposes a limit of an APRES-style restriction on edits to data tables. A change in the prose alone can alter the finding. Preservation and factual verification answer separate questions, because the original claim can itself be wrong.

**For an agent**  
For each changed headline claim, compare the before and after against its ledger entry on the five dimensions. Mark cases that need neighbouring text to resolve. Refer any change in causality, scope, certainty or attribution to the scientific checker and record how it was resolved.

## Jourdan et al. — Reliable metrics for scientific revision

**What it says**  
The authors used 258 original–revised paragraph pairs, two revision instructions per paragraph, six generation models and 1,548 candidate-pair comparisons. Ten NLP researchers judged instruction following, acceptability and preference. Only 129 pairs were double annotated. Agreement on preference was fair, with κ = 0.33. Most conventional similarity metrics ranked copying the original first. Model pairwise judging averaged 56.4% agreement with human extended preferences and reached 82.1% on easy pairs, where only one candidate followed instructions. Performance was weak when both versions were acceptable. ParaPLUIE, a paraphrase metric, reached 55.1% overall. Giving the pairwise judge the authors’ revision scarcely changed agreement. The benchmark measures agreement with a small, specialised panel on paragraph edits. Its acceptability judgement is separate from verifying the science. (Sections 3–8, Tables 2–5 and Figure 7.) [Source](library/evidence/reliable-metrics-scientific-revision-2025.pdf)

**Quotations**  
None selected.

**Bearing on our problem**  
A rewrite that removes accumulated material or changes the order of an argument can score worse on similarity precisely because it makes substantial changes. A model can check an explicit editing instruction more dependably than it can choose the better of two defensible versions.

**Conflicts**  
The paper treats paraphrase detection as a useful measure of meaning preservation. Centonze illustrates how close paraphrases can change the status of a scientific claim. ParaPLUIE may help flag large changes. Claim-level checking is needed for the consequential small ones.

**For an agent**  
Record separate judgements for compliance with the edit request, faithfulness to the evidence and reader preference. Include the unchanged passage as a baseline when testing a revision metric. Allow ties and require a passage-level reason for a preference.

## XtraGPT — Context-aware revision

**What it says**  
XtraGPT revises a selected passage with the full paper, an explicit instruction and section-specific criteria. Its held-out ReviseQA benchmark contained 7,000 model-generated tasks from 350 papers. Removing document context gave the largest penalty in the automated comparison, especially for conclusions. Three research evaluators rated revisions, with a mean willingness to adopt of 3.23/5. A separate before–after assessment of 54 papers raised the automated overall score from 6.08 to 6.73/10. The evidence comes chiefly from AI and machine-learning papers, synthetic revision tasks and model judging. (Sections 2–4, Tables 6 and 8, Limitations.) [Source](library/evidence/xtragpt-2026.pdf)

**Quotations**  
None selected.

**Bearing on our problem**  
A patch can read well in isolation while duplicating an earlier explanation or making the conclusion inconsistent with the results.

**Conflicts**  
PaperMentor benefits from assigning specialists to sections and reports false flags when those specialists lack context. XtraGPT’s context ablation addresses that same trade-off from the revision side.

**For an agent**  
Give the reviser the complete draft and one specific instruction. Check the edited passage against definitions, claims and conclusions elsewhere in the document.

## PaperMentor — Anchored editorial comments

**What it says**  
PaperMentor applies a curated writing skill library to generate comments anchored to LaTeX spans. The study used an 80-paper corpus and 14 annotators, each assigned four papers. They rated comments from PaperMentor and direct prompting with the same GPT-5.2 model under identical presentation. Validity rose from 61.0% to 67.5% and actionability from 86.5% to 90.6%. Conciseness fell from 97.3% to 90.0%. The comparison supports the skill library’s effect on ratings of comments in this setting. It did not test whether acting on those comments improved drafts, and a third of the guided comments were rated invalid. (Sections 4 and 6, Table 2, Limitations.) [Source](library/evidence/papermentor-2026.pdf)

**Quotations**  
None selected.

**Bearing on our problem**  
Anchored comments fit the project’s flag-only roles. Section-specific reviewers can falsely report a missing definition that appears elsewhere, creating pressure to explain it twice.

**Conflicts**  
PaperMentor favours comments the author can act on. APRES applies the edits and selects a revision automatically. PaperMentor tests the feedback. APRES tests preference for the resulting passages.

**For an agent**  
For each comment, give the passage, the reader’s likely difficulty and a concrete action. Search the full draft before flagging an omission. Merge comments that would prompt the same repair.

## Across these sources

- **Two kinds of writing advice have direct experimental support.** Ryba supports revising an abstract as a package to reduce reading difficulty. Readers rated it easier and answered more questions correctly. Feld supports structural editing followed by sentence editing and an author check. Experts found the message easier to locate and gave higher screening ratings. Neither experiment isolates individual devices. Familiar terms, fewer acronyms, explicit links, focused paragraphs and direct verbs are reasonable things to try. The evidence is for the combinations in which they were used.
- **Citation evidence answers a different question.** Hillier and Weinberger associate several forms of connective, concrete or evocative writing with more citations. They differ on abstract length, and Weinberger’s findings on complexity go in a different direction from Ryba’s reader results. The plausible explanation that a detailed abstract is easier to retrieve remains untested here. There is no direct evidence in these sources for a numeral quota, a universal rule on tense or hedges, declarative headings, putting the example first, or a particular way to convey enthusiasm.
- **The project’s strongest unsupported assumption is that a cold model can decide whether a human reader would follow the draft.** A model can help locate an undefined term, a misplaced claim or an instruction that went unfulfilled. Its success or failure on a single reading cannot establish human comprehension. Barry can remain the final editor while readers from each intended audience supply evidence about clarity and enjoyment. The one-writer arrangement and the move from structural outline to fresh prose are also worth testing directly. None of the studies tests those choices on a full ecology article or a two-page brief. [Founding note, lines 138–157 and 213–231](docs/founding-note.md) · [Barry’s methods, lines 8–27](docs/methods/README.md)
- **Compare drafts on three outcomes.** Give different intended readers the original or revision under concealed version labels and the same length budget. After one reading, ask them to state the question, main finding and the condition that most affects its interpretation. Score those answers against the ledger. Separately ask whether they wanted to continue and where their interest declined. Have a scientific checker audit every changed headline claim against the evidence, including quantities, causal force and scope. Retain the diffs and repeat the comparison across Claude, Codex and Gemini. To test accretion specifically, compare a fresh draft from Barry’s outline with a locally revised draft from the same evidence. This would show whether the fresh draft improves reading while preserving the claims.
