**The Expanding Abstract: A method for lean, progressive writing**

This is a new approach, that improves the detailed Structured Writing Method you’ve seen before. The aim here is to avoid the *very* common problem of ‘overwriting’. I asked GPT-4 to define that term:

*Overwriting involves using more words than is necessary, leading to redundancy, filler statements, and overly flowery language. This dilutes the message's impact by obscuring key points with superfluous detail.*

My observation is that most people continue to overwrite. This both wastes the lead authors’ precious time and risks frustrating co-authors who have to plough through and edit the resulting prose. So here I present an ‘expanding abstract’ approach, with some elements from ‘structural planning’.

\[Note: *Make sure you’ve read Brown et al. 1993 “Righting scientific writing”, which is terrific in how it describes how to ‘mind map’, focus strategically on your main message, and write a working abstract*.\]

**Step 0. Mind-Map (the idea machine)**

Mind map (jot thoughts down on a whiteboard or paper) or ‘group plan’ with collaborators (face-to-face or via email or videoconference). As lead author, brainstorm, make notes, collate discussion, and solicit ideas and feedback from co-authors / supervisors. Be free-form and try to avoid self-censoring at this stage.

**Step 1: Answer the Key Questions (the working abstract)**

This sets the context: what you plan to do, why are you doing it, and what gap it fills in the literature (what is the long-standing question?). This working abstract won’t be something you submit for the paper – but it provides the critical context for drafting the paper. It will be a section that you can keep on referring back to as you progress through each step, to keep the main message and research questions firmly in your mind.

<u>The working abstract needs to contain brief answers to the following (~25 words per answer)</u>:

Why are you doing this? \[context and knowledge gap that you are filling – why is this paper needed?\]

What are your aims/research questions? \[should be one core, and maybe multiple sub-goals\]

What are the key hypotheses that underpin your aims/research questions? \[try to avoid ‘silly’ null hypotheses, they should be *a priori* predictions that are grounded in theory.\]

What did you do, or how do you plan to do it? \[methodology: what type of analyses?\]

What did you find? \[core results – say something useful – no filler statements\]

What do these results mean? \[interpretation in context\]

What is it good for \[application\] and how does it apply to a broad readership? How can it be generalised? \[e.g., how is the case study useful when translated to other systems?\]

<u>Display Items</u>: What results are most important to show? Should they be a figure (possibly multi-panel) or a table, or in the supplementary information? Do you need context items? \[e.g., maps to support methods\]

Finally, distil all the above into the paper’s main message (25 words or less). You might have multiple lines of evidence in your paper, *but you should have one main message* (see Brown et al. 1993). If you can’t think of just one, you are either not focussing enough, or you have more than one paper to write.

**Step 2: The One-Page Paper**

All journals impose word limits, be it 8 000, 5 000, or even 3 000 words of main text. The point is, you can never write everything you might want, no matter what the circumstance. *So why not start the process with even stricter constraints?* It is just a different mindset, not a radical change of strategy. Think ‘concise’!

The first task (challenge!) is to write the paper in a <u>single page</u>, ~600 words; about twice that of a typical abstract. A scientific poster is an example of this style, but at this stage you don’t need its figures/tables. If your results are not complete, you can still write up what you have: this approach is inherently modular.

This one-page paper should be essentially understandable as a full MS. All that is needed, but nothing else.

The material you have assembled in step 0/1 will be essential to creating this extended abstract. Work it over, repeatedly, if necessary, until you are <u>completely</u> happy with it. Then solicit feedback from co-authors.

**Step 3: Expansion Stages**

*First Expansion: Section Abstracts*

Next, write 4 × 300-word abstracts (concise but complete summary) of all the key points you will cover in each of the Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion. No more than 300 words. If you can’t fit all that you think you want to say within each section in 300 words, then keep trying until you can. Be ultra strict!

*Second Expansion: Paragraph Abstracts*

Okay, you have created a tight summary of each section. Now it’s time to create the paragraph structure.

First, think carefully about what paragraph framework is required to cover all the material of each section abstract. It should deliver a coherent narrative flow, with each paragraph focusing on a single key message.

Next, for each required paragraph in the section, write single-sentence summary of it. After this, check again that the ordering is right, and that all the material from the section abstract has been covered.

Lastly, for each paragraph, write it as an abstracted version, of 3–4 short, sharp sentences. No more!

Ideally, add sidebar comments to each of these paragraph abstracts, to signpost the references you will cite therein. This ensures that you are incorporating the relevant context and domain knowledge.

At this stage, you should solicit feedback once again from co-authors. But make it clear to them (if they’re unfamiliar with this process) that this is a lean framework of the full paper, not the final product.

*Third Expansion: Full Drafting*

Now you have a tight, complete paper drafted. The structure and flow should all be there, and it ought to make total sense to you. In a way, it’s complete, but it will be lacking the additional details required for a scientific paper. This is the stage that you add that flesh to the bones. But do it judiciously. Don’t overwrite!

**Step 4: Final Detailing**

When the full draft is completed, go back over it to do things like adding references, specific formatting, creating display-item legends etc. Give it another careful read and a hard edit, to cut verbiage. Be concise!

Think of step 3 as building a solid house. Step 4 is the painting and decorating, to make it ready for sale.

**Appendix: General Advice**

*Main message*: Strategic repetition of your main message is key. This means saying the same thing in multiple places using different words (be clever and your readers won’t be aware that you’re doing this).

*Display items:* No strict upper limit but try to have a mix of figures and/or tables, multi-panel figures are okay if they relate to the same theme. Ensure that each legend is stand-alone from the main text.

*Text length:* Default suggestion is to write for the length of a *Proc B*, *Ecol. Lett* or *Ecology* paper. Shorter is far preferable to longer. Typically, 15–30 manuscript pages (max), with 4–6 display items = 3–6 000 words.

Just make sure that you are fully ‘in-the-loop’ and working interactively with it, and critically, ensure it is not overwriting.
