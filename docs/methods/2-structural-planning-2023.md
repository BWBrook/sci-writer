**STRUCTURAL PLANNING (a multi-step process)**

As your supervisors, we (Barry & Jessie) understand the challenges of writing scientific papers, particularly when it comes to organising your thoughts and presenting your research in a coherent and logical manner. To help you navigate this process more effectively, we have developed the guide to streamline the writing process and ensure that your papers are well-structured and impactful.

Below, we outline each step, beginning with the pre-structural planning stage (the DNA) and culminating in the final draft (a well-dressed paper). Each step is designed to help you focus on the critical components of your paper, maintain a clear narrative flow, and facilitate efficient collaboration with your co-authors and supervisors. Please take the time to read this guide and apply its principles to your research papers. In turn, this will allow us to be completely up-to-date with your work and provide you with more timely and staged feedback during your drafting.

**Step 0. Pre-structural plan – conceiving the project and paper**

\[*Make sure you’ve read Brown et al. 1993, which focuses on how to ‘mind map’, focus on your main message, and writing a working abstract. It is the best explanation of these pre-structural methods, so we don’t need to go into further details about that here*.\]

Mind map (jot thoughts down on a whiteboard or paper) and ‘group plan’ with collaborators (face-to-face or via email or videoconference). As first author, brainstorm, make notes, collate discussion. Try to avoid self-censoring at this stage. Ultimately, YOU are the one primarily responsible for then deciding what goes into the paper and what doesn’t.

**Step 1: Cover page (the paper’s DNA)**

This sets the context of the entire paper, what you plan to do, why are you doing it (why is it important?) and what gap it fills in the literature (what is the long-standing question?). This won’t be something you submit for the paper – but it provides the critical context for you to write your paper. It will be a page that you can keep on referring back to as you progress through each step, to keep the main message and research questions firm in your mind.

<u>The cover page needs to contain brief answers to the following (bullet points are fine, but no more than 50 words per answer)</u>:

Why are you doing this? \[context and knowledge gap that you are filling – why is this paper urgent and needed?\]

What are your aims/research questions?

What are the key hypotheses that underpin your aims/research questions? \[Try to avoid ‘silly’ null hypotheses, they should be *a priori* predictions that are grounded in theory.\]

What did you do, or how do you plan to do it? \[Methodology: what type of analyses and display items will you present?\]

If you are at this stage also highlight:

-What did you find? \[core results – say something useful – no motherhood statements\]

-What do these results mean? \[interpretation in context\]

What is it good for \[application\] and how does it apply to an international readership? How can it be generalised (e.g., how is the case study useful when translated to other systems?)

Summarise all the above into a main message (25 words or less).

You may have multiple lines of evidence in your paper, *but you should have one main message* (see Brown et al. 1993). If you can’t think of just one, you are either not focussing enough, or you have more than one paper to write.

**Step 2: Bag of bones**

At this stage, you have a sketch plan (a bag of bones) but are yet to assemble the skeleton. Now it’s time to gather all the pieces and lay them out onto the page in a basic, coherent structure and flow.

a\) Based on your main message and cover page information come up with 3 titles (you can use ChatGPT to help you brainstorm these, it’s great at this sort of creativity).

b\) List authors (think about author order and discuss with supervisor(s) if needed).

\[The person who writes the paper (you) should always be first author. Typically, the supervisor that provides the most advice/feedback should be in the last-author position. Then comes second author in importance, and thereafter the order is usually alphabetical.\]

c\) List some key headings and sub-headings (just make your best guess of the initial structure – remember this is a stage for commenting and changing / rearranging, so we expect considered thought about this, but don’t agonise *too* much over it).

d\) Create the overarching structure and flow of your paper. The way this is achieved is by outlining, *using a single sentence*, what the main message of each *paragraph* is (you can add some minor detail as bullet points \[i.e., explain what you might put in each\], but remember that here the detail is less important). We should be able to read each one-liner, one after the other, to get a feel for the flow and structure of each section, and the overall narrative.

e\) Send it to supervisors/supervisor that will be putting in the most time and effort. Usually, you only need one supervisor to agree at this step (the identified last author).

This is the stage that will likely require the highest number of back-and-forth iterations between you and the supervisor/co-author(s). It is the structural foundation of the paper.

**Step 3: Construct skeleton**

Now that you and your supervisor have agreed on where the pieces might go – it is time to construct the skeleton!

This is the stage where you remove the paragraph context from your bag of bones (step 2) and bullet point the outline of each paragraph. These bullet points should be in narrative order and become the ‘what you did’ text, replacing ‘what you planned to write’.

Then go through another iteration of feedback with supervisor(s)/co-author(s).

\[Note: You should get every relevant co-author to look over and agree on this draft before going to the next step.\]

**  **

**Step 4: Add the flesh**

Now that all your pieces are assembled, your skeleton has been put together, and everyone agrees on its construction, you are ready to add the meat to the bones!

Here you systematically go through and flesh out – with the aid of ChatGPT – the bullet point structure from step 3 into full sentences. Your fleshed-out version should map *exactly* to your agreed upon skeleton (if not questions will be asked, because to do this requires a new skeleton that has been agreed upon). As a rule of thumb, each paragraph should be between 50-250 words, have a beginning, middle and end, and being logically discrete\*. But make sure you retain flow across each paragraph. Here you will also need to be thinking about appropriate references/citation list.

When you write the paragraphs for this step, you can do this in any order you like because you know your structure and flow are already established. This is a great advantage, because some parts of a paper are inevitably easier to write than others (and getting more and more final text down is a psychological boost).

This also punches through writer’s block (as does using AI to help with the initial drafting) and permits you to work on discrete units of your paper to avoid mental burn-out. Don’t try to spend all day writing – take a break with email, a walk, some analysis or coding etc. DO try to set goals for a day (e.g., 5 paragraphs for a day, with an hour on each). Add the references (via Endnote or similar) as you go.

**Step 5: Plastic surgery**

Go through as many drafts (versions of step 4) as needed to tighten the text and get it publication ready. But this is likely to be few if steps 0-3 have been done properly.

Strategic repetition^ of your main message in the Introduction, Results, Discussion and Conclusion is very important. Strategic repetition means saying the same things using different words (be clever and your readers won’t even be aware that you’re doing this).

Circulate the finished draft to your co-authors and give them sufficient time (say two weeks) for feedback. They will be happy to meet this time frame, as they’ve already been embedded in the MS development project (even if it’s just to say “great!” at each juncture in which they’re asked for feedback). Remember, never *demand* feedback from your co-authors. Request it and make the point that it’s optional for any stage *except* comments on the final draft (they need to sign off on it to be included as co-authors). If they give none at a particular stage, it might just be that they’re entirely happy with what you’ve done.

**Appendix: Clothing**

*Display items*

No strict upper limit but try to have a mix of figures and/or tables, multi-panel figures are okay if they relate to the same theme. Create the figures and tables, and write the legends for each. Ensure that each legend is **stand-alone** from the main text.

*Main text length*

Decide on length of main text. Our default suggestion is to write for the length of a *Proc B*, *Ecol Lett* or *Ecology* paper. That is, roughly 30 manuscript pages, or 10 printed pages (as it would appear in the journal). This equates to ~6 pages of text, 2.5 pages of display items, and 1.5 page of references for an average primary data paper. That’s 850 × 6 = ~ 5 000 words of main text. If you need to write more (e.g., detailed methods), it must go in the SI.

Work out the relative size of each section (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusion). A rule of thumb split is 800, 1500, 800, 1700, 200, but it varies depending on how much context setting is required, how many lines of evidence you are using, etc. Yet, despite this, it surprisingly often works out at roughly this ratio.

*Ethical Considerations*

As you work on your scientific papers, it is crucial to adhere to ethical standards in research and writing. Keep the following points in mind to maintain integrity in your work:

1.  Attribution of sources: Always give proper credit to the original authors by citing their work when you refer to or build upon their ideas, data, or findings.

2.  Authorship: Ensure that all individuals who have made significant intellectual contributions to your research project are recognised as authors. Be transparent about each person's contributions, and discuss authorship order with your team to avoid any misunderstandings.

3.  Avoiding plagiarism (including over-use of AI written text): Present your research and ideas in your own words, and be cautious when using direct quotes or paraphrasing the work of others. Make sure to cite your sources appropriately to avoid any unintentional plagiarism.

By upholding these ethical principles, you will contribute to a culture of honesty and integrity within the scientific community, ultimately strengthening the credibility and impact of your research.

**Footnotes: Shoes**

\* By "logically discrete" we mean that each paragraph is a self-contained unit of thought, with a clear and distinct idea or argument. The paragraphs would be separate and independent from one another in terms of logic, yet still contribute to the overall message or purpose of the paper. While the paragraphs may be related to the central theme or topic, they don't necessarily depend on one another for their individual meaning or coherence.

^In Step 5, strategic repetition refers to the intentional restatement of your main message or key points throughout the paper, using different words or phrasing to maintain the reader's interest and reinforce the importance of your findings. Here's an example of strategic repetition for a hypothetical study on the impact of climate change on bird migration patterns:

1.  Introduction: "Our study investigates the effect of climate change on the migratory patterns of bird species, revealing a significant shift in their timing and routes."

2.  Results: "Our analysis indicates that climate change has led to notable alterations in the timing and pathways of bird migration, with many species adapting to new environmental conditions."

3.  Discussion: "The observed changes in bird migration patterns can be largely attributed to the influence of climate change, as it impacts the availability of resources and habitats for these species."

4.  Conclusion: "In conclusion, our findings demonstrate the substantial impact of climate change on bird migratory behaviour, emphasising the need for further research and conservation efforts in this area."

In each instance, the main message (the impact of climate change on bird migration patterns) is restated using different words and phrasing. This strategic repetition helps to reinforce the importance of the research findings and keep the reader focused on the central theme of the paper.
