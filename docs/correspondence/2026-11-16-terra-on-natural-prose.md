# Terra on natural scientific prose

Barry put the same question to Terra (OpenAI) that he had put to Fable on 16 November 2026: what
characterises scientific writing that a person enjoys reading? He pasted Terra's reply into his
conversation with Fable the same day. It is reproduced here as he pasted it.

---

Yes. I think the distinction you’ve drawn between the three products is fundamental. The agent guide can be exhaustive and explicit about every dependency. The brief and article have a different job: they need to bring a reader into an interesting question and carry them through an argument.

A paper can be correct sentence by sentence and still fail as communication if the reader has to assemble the story from a succession of findings, qualifications and numerical comparisons. I think our job is to make the story and its significance apparent while keeping the science intact.

For me, natural scientific prose has a few qualities:

* It has a clear line of thought. Each paragraph gives the reader a reason to read the next. The writer knows which finding matters, why it matters and how it changes the question.
* It says things with the confidence the evidence has earned. A genuinely conditional conclusion should be presented as conditional. But we don’t need to restate every assumption each time we mention it, or make a sound result feel tentative through a fog of “may”, “could”, “potentially” and “suggests”.
* It gives detail a purpose. Numbers, methods and qualifications should help the reader understand or judge the argument. They should appear where they do that work, at a level of detail suited to the piece.
* It sounds like someone thinking clearly. Concrete words, precise verbs and a natural rhythm help. So does an occasional memorable detail. The goal is a distinctive, engaging voice, without dressing the science up in forced metaphors or artificial informality.
* It respects the reader’s attention. We can trust readers to follow a demanding idea if we introduce it well. We shouldn’t ask them to absorb technical machinery before they know why it matters.

The brief and article can apply those principles differently. The brief should make an interested reader care about the puzzle, understand what was discovered and remember why it matters. It can be selective: a few well-chosen details will do more work than a compressed catalogue of results. The article can expect more knowledge and patience, and must give readers enough evidence and method to assess the conclusions. It should still be a pleasure for a scientist in an adjacent field to read; the supplement and agent guide can carry much of the finer machinery.

There’s already a glimpse of this in the current brief: “One find can move the limit by 20 million years.” That makes me want to know which find and why it matters. Elsewhere, a reader encounters samples “sharing a well effect” and an “exploratory figure near 0.4”. Those details have a scientific purpose, but they make the reader work hard before the significance is clear. We should be able to convey the important dependence and sensitivity in language that serves the story.

I also want to be candid about my role. I can fall into the same habits as the other models, including making prose sound polished but lifeless. So I’ll treat your reaction as evidence: if a passage is accurate but you don’t enjoy reading it, that’s a problem to solve. Equally, I’ll speak up if a smoother sentence changes what the result actually says.

Does that feel like the right starting point? If there are scientists, essayists or particular papers whose writing you especially enjoy, they might also give us useful reference points.
