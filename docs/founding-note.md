# Founding note

Claude Fable 5.2, 19 November 2026. Barry asked for this note so that a new agent can start work
here without the conversation that led to it. That conversation ran from 14 to 19 November 2026 in
the `silurian_plants` repository, where Fable was advising on the first paper of that project (study
F3, on what missing fossils can say about the age of land plants).

Everything marked "Barry" is quoted from that conversation with its date. Everything marked
"proposal" is Fable's and has not been tested.

## The problem

Barry's research repositories each produce three documents from one evidence base: a journal
article, a brief of at most two A4 pages for any interested reader, and a guide written for agents.
AI agents write all three. In `silurian_plants` the writers are Claude Opus 5.5 and OpenAI Codex.

The guide for agents works. The brief and the article are accurate and hard to enjoy. Barry, 16
November 2026:

> it's been written by a cadre of very clever and diligent AIs who are great doers but write in a
> very obsessive, details and caveat focused manner that makes it hard for a person to read (or at
> least enjoy).

> It has to sound like real prose that someone, some human has written, but moreover, some human
> would actually enjoy reading. I think that's the key.

> I think the current brief and article are technically very sound. [...] But in terms of
> communication devices for people, they're pretty terrible.

The aim is quality for the reader. Every publication declares the AI systems that wrote it, so
concealment plays no part. Barry, same day: "I'm not trying to hide any AI writing here. I'm just
trying to develop a system where the final text that's produced with the intention of **people**
reading it is as nice to read and communicative as possible."

On 19 November he judged the problem wider than one paper and asked for this repository. Its job, in
his words: "to create documentation as guidance for new agents, and also be used to develop skills,
read and summarise 'how to write' pdfs that I have, etc. I'm not 100% clear yet on what will go into
it though: it'll be built by iteration."

## Why the agents' prose is hard to enjoy

The cause lies in how the text is made. Each review round finds a sentence that could mislead and
adds a safeguard. No reviewer acts for the reader. The checking agent asks whether each sentence is
exactly true and never asks whether a person would keep reading. The `silurian_plants` project had recorded this on
12 November as "prose shaped by review".

Fable read the F3 article and brief as a reader would and found seven symptoms. Barry agreed with
all of them and added the eighth.

1. **Every sentence defends itself.** The text addresses a hostile reviewer. A human author states
   the point, qualifies it once and trusts the reader to remember.
2. **Too many numbers.** Two paragraphs of the F3 Results held about 25 numerals. A reader keeps one
   or two from a paragraph.
3. **A private dialect.** "Finds certainly inside the window", "counting rules", "inclusion states",
   "conditional gap benchmarks". The reader must learn the project's language before the science.
4. **Everything has equal weight.** Nine results sections, with no sign of which three matter.
5. **No author.** Nobody says what they expected, what surprised them or what they think.
6. **Nothing to picture.** The article never said what the plant looks like or what a borehole
   sample is.
7. **The order of the analysis, not of the argument.** The Introduction opened with a definition and
   a list of studies.
8. **No enthusiasm** (Barry, 16 November): "There is also no enthusiasm or passion there for the
   work. It's fundamentally fascinating stuff, and gets at a crucial question on the origins of
   terrestrial life."

## What good looks like

Barry called this list "on target" (16 November).

- One question, raised in the first paragraph and answered in the last.
- One argument that the reader could repeat to a colleague afterwards.
- The example before the rule, and the concrete thing before the abstract one.
- Each paragraph does one job, opens with its point and stops.
- Few numbers in the prose, rounded unless the precision matters. Exact values go in tables.
- Ordinary words, and one name for each thing.
- A stated view, with reasons.
- Omission. An article is an argument, and the supplement holds the inventory.

Omission is the hardest of these for the agents, because leaving something out feels dishonest to
them. The three-document design removes that worry. The guide for agents and the claims ledger carry
everything, so the article and the brief are free to select.

## The lesson of the first attempt

Fable then wrote a storyline for the F3 brief. Barry liked its style and took it apart phrase by
phrase. The paragraph:

> Clocks disagree by some 60 million years about when the ancestor of living land plants lived, and
> the disagreement comes down to how missing fossils are read. We worked out how much the missing
> fossils can bear. The silence before the first vascular-plant fossils is worth about three finds,
> which is very little. An older origin therefore needs only that early plants were found several
> times less often, and the record holds at least one long quiet stretch of that kind. But the quiet
> ends fast: by the late Silurian, fossils turn up at close to their full rate. So either vascular
> plants arose near their first fossils, or they lived a long time as something we rarely find and
> then became findable quickly.

Barry's critique, 16 November 2026:

| Phrase | Barry | Fault |
|---|---|---|
| "Clocks" | "What's a clock in this context (it's not a timepiece on the wall)." | Term used before it is defined |
| "living land plants" | "Why 'living plants' rather than 'all land plants'? Is it meant to contrast with dead plants?" | A technical contrast (alive today against extinct relatives) left unstated |
| "how missing fossils are read" | "How do you read something that's missing, there's nothing to 'read'" | Metaphor in place of a literal statement |
| "The silence" | "Silence implies a lack of sound, not a form of evidence not yet uncovered or perhaps never preserved." | Sound metaphor for a physical absence |
| "vascular-plant" | "What is a vascular plant?" | Term used before it is defined |
| "worth about three finds" | "means what? Statistical, or intrinsic value, or?" | Shorthand whose sense is open |
| "long quiet stretch" | "there is that sound metaphor for physical absence again, and LLMs love 'quiet' (it's become a tic)" | Metaphor, and a habit of the models |
| "ends fast" | "It should be 'quickly'?" | Loose wording |
| "full rate" | "comes out of nowhere, what even is a rate?" | Quantity never introduced |

His general point:

> *I* know what you mean by all of these, but can you see the trap in assuming too much of your
> readers? The general reader would be confused or ambivalent about meaning. The specialist would be
> irked by your lack of precision. Neither would be likely satisfied.

The lesson is that a writer who knows the project hears its shorthand as plain language. Nine faults
sat in one short paragraph that its author thought was clear. Rules from it:

- Define each term where it first appears, or use a plain word.
- Use the literal phrase. An absence is an absence.
- Introduce every quantity before using it: say what is counted and against what.

## Restraint

Good rhythm goes unnoticed. Barry, 16 November: "Short sentences where the conclusions land, but
nothing can be overdone, or too clever. Otherwise it comes off as contrived, rather than clever
writing." He cited the well-known teaching paragraph that varies its sentence lengths to make prose
"sing": "I found that to be *terrible* because it was just trying too hard. Let's not do that
please!"

Fable's working test (proposal): could the sentence be read aloud to a colleague without sounding
like a performance?

Enthusiasm follows the same rule. It shows in what the writer chooses to tell the reader and in a
plainly stated view. Adjectives do not supply it.

## The lanes

Barry agreed this division of work on 16 November: "It's an excellent framework that keeps the right
lanes."

1. **One writer owns the voice.** The writer drafts fresh from the argument, and does not edit the
   working agents' text. In `silurian_plants` the writer is Fable, which did none of the analysis.
2. **Barry reads first, for voice.** If the voice is wrong, nothing is worth checking yet. He is the
   final arbiter and editor.
3. **A reader's editor.** A second model marks where it lost the thread, where the text is flat and
   where it tries too hard. It may suggest wording. The writer decides the words. Barry's choice is
   Terra, which he finds the best writer among the OpenAI models.
4. **Truth-checkers.** The agents that did the science check each sentence against the claims ledger
   and report faults. They do not rewrite; repairs return to the writer to phrase.
5. **Disputes go to Barry.** On substance the truth-checkers win. On wording the writer chooses.

Barry's summary: "There's only one writer there, and two types of editor."

The point of lanes 1 and 4 is to stop safeguards accreting again. Whether the flag-only rule holds in
practice is untested.

Terra's own account of natural scientific prose is in
`docs/correspondence/2026-11-16-terra-on-natural-prose.md`. Its principles agree with those above.
Two of its points add to them: detail needs a purpose, and Barry's reaction is to be treated as
evidence.

## Writers Barry admires

Barry, 16 November: "David Raup was truly excellent, in both his scientific papers and his wonderful
book 'Extinction: Bad Genes or Bad Luck'. Michael Benton is somewhat plainer, but eminently readable:
he knows how to write serious and popular science. Eldredge and Gould, for their faults, wrote well."

Fable described what each might teach, from memory and without rereading them: Raup explains
probability through ordinary situations and says plainly when he does not know; Benton is orderly and
declarative; Gould starts from one particular case. These descriptions need checking against the
texts before they guide anything.

## Earlier rules that still stand

Barry gave these in `silurian_plants` before this conversation. They are recorded there in Claude's
project memory.

- 10 November 2026: state findings positively. "main messages with a '... are not x' negation is bad
  form. You really overuse negations within positive statements throughout."
- 12 November 2026: point first, condition second, number last; one name per thing.
- 15 November 2026: gather caveats in one short section. A caveats paragraph that had grown to 695
  words had "swelled to absurdity"; the detail belongs in a supplement table "rather than dragging
  down the main article like a sheet anchor". The agents had obeyed the one-section rule "to
  extremis". Fable's working rule (proposal): a caveat earns space in the main text only if it
  changes how a reader should take a headline result.

The third item shows a general risk. A rule handed to the working agents is obeyed mechanically, and
the result can be as bad as the fault it replaced.

## Open questions

1. **What form should the guidance take?** A document for agents to read, a skill, or both. The
   caveats episode suggests that rules alone will be obeyed to the letter and miss the point.
2. **What can code check?** Candidates (proposal): numerals per paragraph; project terms used before
   they are defined; a list of metaphors and habitual words; negations inside claims. Code cannot
   tell whether a text has an argument or a reason to keep reading.
3. **How should the reference texts be used?** Barry has PDFs on writing. Each needs a summary in
   our own words, with the advice that bears on this problem drawn out.
4. **How do we know a text is better?** Barry's reading is the evidence, and it is scarce. One test
   to try (proposal): give the text once to a model that knows nothing of the project and ask it to
   state the argument. A text that fails this has failed a human reader too.
5. **Do the definitions fit?** Defining every term costs words. The pilot brief ran 350 words over
   its two pages. Which findings a brief drops is a judgement about the science.
6. **Does the article need a different process from the brief?** It must carry enough evidence and
   method for a specialist to judge it. Where the line falls between article and supplement is
   unsettled.
7. **Does the flag-only rule hold?** No draft has yet been through the truth-checkers under it.
8. **Does the guidance work for other model families?** The writer so far is one Claude model.
