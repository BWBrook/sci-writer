# Ten devices that AI writers overuse

Claude Fable 5.2, 19 November 2026. Barry supplied a table of ten habits of current AI prose, of
unknown authorship and circulating online. The image is not committed. The ten categories are
restated here in our own words, and each example is taken from a text in this repository, with its
file named. Fable wrote every example except where stated.

## How to use this list

Barry, 19 November 2026: "All are valid writing methods, but rendered comical by a recent history of
AI overuse (as was the now-famed em-dash)." None of the ten is banned. Each is a device that a good
writer uses now and then for a reason. The fault is frequency, and use without a purpose.

His guiding rule, same day: "everything is written in a careful, deliberative and measured hand,
with a [view] to communicative scientific clarity rather than an inclination towards rhetorical
flourish." And: "great care must always be made to moderate such stylistic tricks."

A writer who finds one of these in a draft asks what the device does for the reader at that place.
If the plain sentence would serve as well, the plain sentence is used. A second use of the same
device on a page needs a better reason than the first.

These ten belong to the terse, analytical voice of a model that has been told to be direct. The
`audit-ai-writing` skill mainly describes an older voice (inflated significance, stock vocabulary,
"not only X but also Y"), and a search of it on 19 November found six of the ten absent. The two
lists complement each other.

## The ten

### 1. Denial before the claim

The writer says what a thing is not, then what it is, or states the finding only as a denial. Used
once, it corrects a belief the reader really holds. Used often, the reader is told about many errors
they never made. Barry had objected to it on 10 November (see `docs/founding-note.md`).

> A reader told to list every unclear phrase lists about that many whatever it is given, so the
> number of flags is not a score.
> (`examples/silurian-brief/cold-reads/README.md`)

### 2. Announcing importance

A sentence is built to tell the reader that this is the part that matters, often as "it is X that"
or "what did Y was Z". It helps when a long passage needs one point lifted above the rest. As a
habit it replaces showing why the point matters.

> What moved the answer by 55 million years was a judgement about missing fossils
> (`examples/silurian-brief/samples-article.md`)

### 3. An abstraction as the actor

A statistic, a table, a record or a limit is made the subject of an active verb. "The data show" is
ordinary scientific usage. The device becomes a habit when abstractions hide, point, hold, bear and
tell, and no person does anything.

> The limit of 515.5 million years points to older sea-floor rocks that preserve the tough cysts of
> algae and no plant spores.
> (`examples/silurian-brief/draft-1.md`. The authors of the limit did the pointing.)

### 4. The pointer before the thing

The sentence refers by a count or a pronoun to things the reader must go back and identify. It saves
words where the things were named a moment ago. Otherwise the reader does the writer's work.

> Three arguments of this kind account for most of the disagreement between the estimates. We tested
> them.
> (`examples/silurian-brief/draft-1.md`. The three arguments have not been named at this point.)

### 5. A heading that teases or argues

One kind of heading withholds its subject to raise curiosity. Another states a conclusion. Barry,
19 November 2026: "Declarative finding headings are okay, again if used with care and purpose." A
heading that names its section plainly is always available.

> The lesson of the first attempt
> (`docs/founding-note.md`. The reader cannot tell what the section is about.)

> Once vascular plants appear, they are found often.
> (`examples/silurian-brief/draft-1.md`. A declarative finding. The draft has five in a row.)

### 6. The maxim at the end

A paragraph closes on a short general saying. It suits the rare point that the reader should carry
away. When paragraph after paragraph ends this way, the text reads as a performance, which is
Barry's objection under "Restraint" in the founding note.

> An absence is an absence.
> (`docs/founding-note.md`)

> The cost is length.
> (`examples/silurian-brief/README.md`)

### 7. The wrong reading, raised to be dismissed

The writer states an interpretation that nobody has offered and then rejects it. It is fair when the
mistaken reading is common and named. We found no full case in our texts. The nearest answers an
accusation the reader had not made:

> Every publication declares the AI systems that wrote it, so concealment plays no part.
> (`docs/founding-note.md`)

### 8. A run of fragments

Verbless phrases or very short sentences are set in a row for rhythm. One short sentence can mark a
conclusion: Barry, 16 November 2026, "Short sentences where the conclusions land, but nothing can be
overdone". A row of them is the cadence he called contrived.

> **No author.** [...] **Nothing to picture.** [...] **No enthusiasm**
> (`docs/founding-note.md`, the list of symptoms)

> The genomes were identical.
> (`examples/silurian-brief/samples-article.md`. Fable marked this one itself when it wrote it.)

### 9. A metaphor where a literal phrase exists

An image stands in for a statement that could be made directly. A metaphor earns its place when it
explains something the literal phrase cannot, as the doctors' rule of three does in `draft-1.md`.
Barry's critique of 16 November rejected three of this kind in one paragraph.

> The silence before the first vascular-plant fossils is worth about three finds, which is very
> little.
> (the storyline paragraph, quoted in `docs/founding-note.md`)

### 10. A label before the sentence

A bold phrase or a phrase and colon is put in front of a sentence that could have stood alone. It
helps a reader scan a reference list or a set of instructions. In an article or a brief it turns
prose into slides.

> **One writer owns the voice.** The writer drafts fresh from the argument
> (`docs/founding-note.md`. Most of Fable's bullets in this repository take this form.)

## Words to watch

The same caution applies to single words that the models overuse. Barry has named two so far.

- "quiet", for an absence or a lull. Barry, 16 November 2026: "LLMs love 'quiet' (it's become a
  tic)".
- "-shaped", as a suffix that makes an adjective of any noun. Barry, 19 November 2026: "'shaped' is
  another one on the list". The table Barry supplied used it in one of its own category names.
