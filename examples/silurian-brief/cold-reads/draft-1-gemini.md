### 1. The argument (in three sentences)

Scientists disagree by 65 million years over when the ancestor of today's land plants lived, largely because molecular-clock estimates use "missing fossils" to rule out older dates—assuming that if early plants had existed in older rocks, we would have found them. Testing those limits shows that missing fossils are much weaker evidence than advertised: the oldest limit has never been quantified, spores missing from one region existed elsewhere at the same time, and for early vascular plants the fossil record only rules out an older origin if early plants were found as easily as later ones. Because the fossils fit either a younger origin or a 20-million-year hidden history where early plants were roughly ten times harder to find, molecular-clock studies need to state and test their assumptions about fossil discovery rather than treating missing fossils as hard limits.

### 2. The opening question, and whether the end answers it

The opening asks how old the most recent common ancestor of *all* living land plants is—currently estimated anywhere from 515 to 450 million years ago—and promises to test the three missing-fossil arguments behind that disagreement.

The end only half-answers it, and quietly swaps the subject. It answers the subtitle ("what missing fossils can tell us": not a hard age limit, only a conditional assumption about preservation and discovery), but "What it means" talks solely about *vascular plants* and never returns to the 515–450 million-year range for all land plants or says what the tests mean for the first two limits.

### 3. Unclear terms and shifting names

- **"oldest possible age" / "oldest limit" / "the limit" / "second limit" / "published limit"** — The same concept (an upper age bound for a molecular clock) shifts names repeatedly without saying they are equivalent, and it is not always clear which plant group each limit applies to.
- **"tough cysts of algae"** — I do not know what an algal cyst is, or why finding them in sea-floor rocks is supposed to show that land-plant spores would have been preserved there too.
- **"The second limit, 459.1 million years, is set by the oldest spores with a three-rayed scar"** — I do not know what a three-rayed scar signifies or which plant group it marks, and since earlier a found fossil set a *youngest* possible age, it is confusing how found spores set a limit that is then tested by *missing* spores.
- **"that basin" (vs. "one place" / "Libya")** — "Basin" arrives as unexplained geological jargon (a valley, a sea floor, a rock formation?) and appears to be another word for the Libyan region just mentioned.
- **"vascular plants" (after "land plants")** — The brief opens on all land plants (explicitly including mosses) and then pivots to vascular plants (which exclude mosses) without explaining how a limit on vascular plants connects to the 515–450 million-year debate over all land plants.
- **"counting each place and age once" / "same beds" / "independent discoveries" / "a more cautious count of the finds" / "depending on how the finds are counted"** — One methodological idea (de-duplicating fossils from the same rock layers, where "beds" is also unexplained jargon) goes by five different, increasingly vague labels.
- **"exclude only about two million years" / "we can exclude only an earlier history long enough to have produced about three"** — This reads backwards against the drug-trial analogy: if zero bad reactions in 300 patients leaves rates *up to* 3 in 300 possible and excludes rates *higher* than that, then zero older fossils should leave the earlier 2 million years *open* and exclude histories *longer* than 2 million years.
- **"whose place among vascular plants is uncertain"** — If *Cooksonia* may not even be a vascular plant, I cannot tell why an Irish *Cooksonia* was used to set the published limit for vascular plants, or what defines the "wider group of simple branched plants."
- **"plants recognisable as vascular appeared 15 to 23 million years after the ancestor lived"** — It is puzzling how the ancestor of vascular plants could live 15–23 million years before plants recognisable as vascular existed, without explaining that its defining traits may have evolved or become preservable later.
- **"one plant body, some fragments of spore capsules"** — Earlier the brief contrasts "spores" with a "plant body", so calling loose fragments of spore capsules a "plant body" feels inconsistent, and "finds" throughout blurs bodies, fragments, and different plant groups.
- **"the period that follows"** — Neither the dates nor the length of this later period are given, so I cannot tell what the 75 finds are being compared against or why the baseline suddenly shifts to "more than 454 million years ago" instead of 459.1.
- **"the late Silurian"** — Even with dates next to it, "Silurian" is unexplained geological terminology dropped in once near the end.

### 4. Where I lost the thread or wanted to stop reading

- **The Libya / 459.1 million-year paragraph:** I lost the logic immediately because the limit is introduced via *found* spores in Sweden, yet tested via *missing* spores in younger-and-older rocks in Libya, with no explanation of why three-rayed spores matter or how Sweden's limit relied on absence.
- **The abrupt shift to vascular plants (Finding 3):** Expecting the third of the three arguments behind the 515–450 million-year range for all land plants, I was thrown when the brief dropped to 431.2 million years for a subgroup without bridging the two questions.
- **The rule-of-three and *Cooksonia* thicket (Findings 3–4):** This is where I wanted to stop reading—more than twenty numbers arrive in quick succession, the wording of "exclude" appears to invert the medical analogy, and the detour through Irish vs. Czech *Cooksonia* and 69 finds in a "wider group" reads like a footnote for specialists.
- **The bounce to Oman and back to vascular plants (end of Finding 4 and Finding 5):** Jumping from vascular plants back to "land plants as a whole" (with a new unexplained 454 Ma date and 75 finds) and then straight back to vascular plants (with an unexplained "4 to 15 million years" transition) broke the momentum right before the conclusion.

### 5. What to cut, and the one finding to keep

- **Cut to make it shorter:**
  1. **The Irish/Czech *Cooksonia* and "wider group" paragraph** (end of Finding 3): it adds taxonomic confusion and four extra numbers without changing the argument.
  2. **Finding 5 ("Once vascular plants appear, they are found often"):** its statistical detail ("13 of 48", "two-thirds", "4 to 15 million years") slows the ending down, and its only essential idea—that a long hidden history requires a sudden shift to becoming common—is already stated more clearly in "What it means."
  3. **The secondary "cautious count" variants** ("24 independent discoveries", "2.4 to 4.5 times"): keeping one clean count per point would cut half the numerical clutter.
- **Keep only one finding:** **Finding 4 ("An older ancestor requires early plants to have been found about ten times less often").** It is the single result that directly bridges the gap between the fossils and the older molecular-clock dates, turning a vague excuse ("maybe we missed them") into a concrete, testable number—roughly a tenfold jump in how rarely early plants were found, or a 15–23 million-year wait before they became recognisable.
