# How old is the ancestor of today's land plants? What missing fossils can tell us

## The question

Every moss, fern, tree and crop alive today descends from one ancestral species of small plant. Its
descendants changed the air, the soils and the climate, so the date at which it lived sets the timetable
for those changes. Recent estimates of that date range from 515 to 450 million years ago.

The estimates come from a method called a molecular clock. DNA changes slowly from generation to
generation, so the more the DNA of two living species differs, the longer ago their lineages separated.
To turn DNA differences into years, the method needs fossils of known age. A fossil gives a youngest
possible age for its group, because the group existed by the time that plant died. The oldest possible
age is harder to set. It has to be argued from fossils that are missing: had the group existed much
earlier, the argument runs, someone would have found a trace of it in older rocks. Three arguments of
this kind account for most of the disagreement between the estimates. We tested them.

The ancestor in question is the most recent one shared by all land plants alive today. Earlier relatives
that left no living descendants may have reached land before it.

## Why early plant fossils are scarce

The earliest land plants were tiny and had no wood. The best known, *Cooksonia*, was a leafless stalk
about a millimetre wide that forked two or three times and carried a capsule of spores at each tip. Its
fossils are fragments a few centimetres long. Spores are the microscopic, tough-walled cells by which
such plants reproduce, and they survive in rock far more readily than the plants do. Spores appear tens
of millions of years before any plant body.

When older rocks hold no plant fossils, then, two explanations are possible. Either the plants did not
yet exist, or they existed and were seldom preserved, found and recognised. An oldest possible age has
to argue for the first explanation.

## What we found

**The oldest limit rests on an argument that has never been measured.** The limit of 515.5 million years
points to older sea-floor rocks that preserve the tough cysts of algae and no plant spores. Nobody has
measured how many spores would have reached those seas, or how thoroughly the rocks were searched.

**A fossil missing from one place can be present in another.** The second limit, 459.1 million years, is
set by the oldest spores with a three-rayed scar, found in Sweden. In rock samples from boreholes in Libya, none of 28
samples older than about 443 million years is reported to contain such spores, while 51 of 59 younger
samples are. Most of those older Libyan samples date from times when three-rayed spores were already
present elsewhere. The plants existed, and their spores were absent from that basin or too few to be
seen. The absence of spores from one basin is therefore weak evidence that the plants were absent
everywhere.

**For vascular plants, the missing fossils exclude only about two million years.** Vascular plants have
internal tubes that carry water. Clubmosses, ferns and seed plants are the living kinds. The limit for
their ancestor is 431.2 million years. The published record holds 48 finds of early vascular plants,
counting each place and age once. The oldest is at most 426.7 million years old, and the 48 are spread
over the following 33 million years, which is one find for about every 700,000 years.

Suppose vascular plants had lived before the oldest find and had been found just as often. Every further
700,000 years should then have produced about one more find, and none is known. Doctors have a rule for
this situation, called the rule of three. If 300 patients take a drug and none reacts badly, the true
rate of bad reactions may still be as high as 3 in 300; that is the most the trial can exclude with 95%
confidence. The same arithmetic applies to fossils. With no older finds, we can exclude only an earlier history long enough to have produced about
three. At the observed pace that is about 2 million years, which puts the limit at 428.8 million years.
If the 48 finds are really 24 independent discoveries, because several come from the same beds, three
finds take twice as long and the limit falls almost exactly on the published 431.2.

That published limit was set from an Irish *Cooksonia*. *Cooksonia* belongs to a wider group of simple
branched plants whose place among vascular plants is uncertain. The wider group has 69 finds, the oldest
of them a Czech *Cooksonia* up to 432.9 million years old, and the same arithmetic gives it a limit of
434 to 437 million years.

**An older ancestor requires early plants to have been found about ten times less often.** Some clock
estimates put the ancestor of vascular plants near 450 million years ago, more than 20 million years
before the oldest find. The fossil record fits that date if early vascular plants were found 8 to 12 times less
often than later ones, or 2.4 to 4.5 times on a more cautious count of the finds. It also fits if plants
recognisable as vascular appeared 15 to 23 million years after the ancestor lived.

The record of land plants as a whole requires a difference of the same size. Spores show that land plants
existed more than 454 million years ago. The next 21 million years have yielded one plant body, some
fragments of spore capsules from a borehole in Oman, against 75 finds from the period that follows. Over
those 21 million years, bodies of land plants were found at least 8 times less often than afterwards, or
became recognisable only later.

**Once vascular plants appear, they are found often.** At least 13 of the 48 finds come from the late
Silurian, 427 to 420 million years ago, and from six regions of the world. Vascular plants were being
found then at two-thirds or more of their average pace over the whole 33 million years. If they had
existed long before, the change from seldom found to often found took between about 4 and 15 million
years, depending on how the finds are counted.

## What it means

Two accounts fit this evidence. In the first, vascular plants arose within a few million years of their
oldest fossils, as the youngest clock estimates say. In the second, they lived for 20 million years or
more as plants that were seldom preserved or seldom recognisable, and then became common in the rocks
over a much shorter time. The second account needs a cause for that change. A new feature may have let
the plants spread, they may have moved into places where fossils form, or the conditions of preservation
may have changed. The fossils known today fit both accounts, and our calculations state what each one
requires.

For those who build molecular clocks, an oldest possible age drawn from missing fossils is an assumption
about how often early organisms were preserved and found. Stating that assumption and testing others
would show how much of each date comes from the DNA and how much from the assumption.
